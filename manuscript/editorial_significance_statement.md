# Editorial significance statement

Coastal flood screening often uses extreme water-level indicators as if they directly represented flood susceptibility. This manuscript shows why that shortcut can be misleading. By combining open surge reconstruction, storm-tide return-level, meteorological-driver and DeltaDTM terrain data, the study separates three diagnostic dimensions: water-level product definition, atmospheric coherence and ocean-connected lowland terrain sensitivity.

The key finding is not simply that two water-level products differ. They represent different physical quantities and should differ in many settings. The more important result is that water-level magnitude and terrain susceptibility can be spatially decoupled. At the five European terrain-screening sites analysed here, Sheerness shows the strongest ocean-connected +2 m lowland sensitivity, while Hoek van Holland, Newlyn, Brest and Aberdeen remain below 10% after isolated low cells are excluded by a boundary-connected mask.

The study therefore contributes a reproducible process-terrain screening framework: one that keeps physical quantities distinct, uses real open datasets, records figure source data, and explicitly avoids overclaiming static terrain sensitivity as hydrodynamic flood prediction. This approach is relevant to coastal scientists, hazard analysts and data-product users who need transparent first-order screening before investing in local dynamic modelling.

## Short version

This study reframes coastal flood screening as a coupling problem between water-level processes and connected lowland terrain. It shows that extreme water-level indicators alone can miss important terrain-mediated susceptibility differences, and it provides a reproducible open-data workflow for diagnosing that mismatch.
